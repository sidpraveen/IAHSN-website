What Is HS?

Hidradenitis Suppurativa

Pronounced: hide-ra-den-eye-tis sup-you-rah-tee-vah.

Understanding HS

Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic, recurrent inflammatory skin disease that can cause painful, deep-seated nodules and abscesses. It commonly affects the armpits, groin, buttocks, and skin beneath the breasts.

HS begins in hair follicles. Follicular blockage and an abnormal inflammatory response are involved, but the exact cause is not fully understood. HS is not caused by poor hygiene. Treatment addresses the underlying inflammation as well as skin symptoms.

How is HS diagnosed?

A healthcare professional considers three main characteristics:

  • The type of lesions, such as deep-seated nodules, abscesses, tunnels, or scars.
  • Their location, commonly in the armpits, groin, buttocks, or beneath the breasts.
  • Whether the lesions recur or persist over time.

HS can be mistaken for boils, common abscesses, ingrown hairs, or skin infections. A dermatologist can help evaluate recurring symptoms and discuss appropriate care.

Questions to discuss with your healthcare provider:

  • Does anyone else in your family experience similar symptoms?
  • Do the nodules recur in the same area?
  • Do symptoms worsen before or during your menstrual cycle?

Hurley stages

The Hurley system describes the extent of HS in an affected skin region. These stages are a clinical assessment, not a self-diagnosis tool.

Stage I

Single or multiple abscesses without tunnels or scarring.

Stage II

Recurring abscesses with tunnels and scarring, with separated areas of involvement.

Stage III

Widespread involvement with multiple interconnected tunnels and abscesses across an affected area.

HS does not follow the same course for everyone. It can worsen over time, but progression to severe disease is not inevitable. Your healthcare provider can assess your symptoms and help plan ongoing care.

Who gets HS?

  • Sex: HS is more common in women; U.S. estimates are about three women for every man.
  • Race: In the United States, HS is diagnosed more commonly in Black people than in white people.
  • Genetics: HS can run in families. About one-third of people with HS have a relative with the condition.
  • Age: HS often begins around puberty or in early adulthood, but it can also develop later in life.
